It's one of the questions we hear most often at the clinic, and it almost always has a way forward if you handle it calmly.

This guide doesn't replace a visit or the leaflet that comes with your pack. It helps you understand how the pill fits into daily life, what to do when your routine slips and when you need help right away.

## The pill works through consistency, not strength

The birth control pill protects you when you take it every day, ideally around the same time. You don't need to take it with food or on an empty stomach, but the habit has to be solid: how well it works in real life depends far more on not skipping doses than on anything else.

So before choosing it, ask yourself honestly whether your routine lets you remember every single day. If your schedule shifts a lot, it can still work; you just need a system that doesn't rely on memory.

## Tricks that actually help you remember

- **A fixed alarm on your phone**, with a label only you understand if privacy worries you.
- **Tie it to something you never skip**, like brushing your teeth before bed or your first coffee.
- **Keep the pack in sight**, next to your toothbrush or on the nightstand, not buried in a drawer.
- **A spare pack in your bag** if you often sleep away from home or work long shifts.
- **Mark on your calendar when the next pack starts**, so you ask for a new prescription in time and never run out.

Some packs include pills of a different color for the last days, or a break between one pack and the next. Each kind is used differently, so read the leaflet in your box and, if anything is unclear, ask us before you start.

## I missed a pill: now what?

Take a breath. One missed pill doesn't automatically mean pregnancy, but it does call for a few steps in order:

1. **Get out the leaflet from your pack.** Each type of pill comes with its own instructions for missed doses, and they differ from one product to another. It explains what to do depending on how many pills you skipped and which week of the pack you're in.
2. **Use a backup method**, such as condoms, every time you have sex until your situation is clear. It's the simplest way to avoid risk in the meantime.
3. **Call or message us.** The medical team at Clínica Hispana Nueva Salud will guide you on how to continue the pack and how long to keep using extra protection.
4. **If you had unprotected sex during those days**, or your period doesn't come when expected, take a [pregnancy test](/en/services/prueba-embarazo); clearing up the doubt lets you plan your next step with peace of mind.

Some situations count as a missed pill even if you did swallow it: vomiting shortly after taking it, severe diarrhea, or starting a new medication that might make it less effective. In those cases, let us know as well, and keep using backup until we talk it through.

## What you may notice at the start

During the first months your body is adjusting. Here is what tends to show up, and what should never wait:

| Common in the first months | Signs that mean calling 911 |
| --- | --- |
| Spotting or light bleeding between periods | Strong pain or swelling in one leg, especially the calf |
| Tender or slightly swollen breasts | Chest pain or sudden shortness of breath |
| Mild nausea, mostly when taken on an empty stomach | A sudden, severe headache unlike your usual ones |
| A mild headache or mood changes | Loss of vision, trouble speaking or weakness on one side of the body |
| Shorter or lighter periods | Bleeding so heavy it makes you dizzy or faint |

The items on the left usually ease over the weeks. If they don't improve, bother you a lot, or the irregular bleeding keeps going, come to a [birth control](/en/services/anticonceptivos) visit: part of the follow-up is adjusting the method when something doesn't suit you. The items on the right are not for waiting until an appointment: call 911 or go to the emergency room. The same goes if you think you might be pregnant and have strong pain on one side of your belly, or bleeding.

## When the pill may not be right for you

Not every pill is safe for every woman. At the visit we check your blood pressure and your history, because some conditions change the recommendation:

- **High blood pressure**, especially if it isn't under control. If you already have [blood pressure follow-up](/en/services/condiciones-cronicas) with us, bring your readings.
- **Migraines**, particularly those that come with lights or spots in your vision before the pain.
- **Smoking**, because the risk of blood clots goes up, more so with age.
- **Breastfeeding**, since not every type of pill is recommended while you nurse.
- **A history of blood clots** in the legs or lungs, yours or in close relatives.

None of this gets decided online. Share it honestly at the visit and, if the pill turns out not to be the best choice, we'll walk you through other options, such as the shot, which is given right at the clinic.
