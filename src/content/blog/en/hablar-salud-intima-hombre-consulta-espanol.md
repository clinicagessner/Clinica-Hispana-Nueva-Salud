Don Arturo had been carrying something for almost a year without telling anyone. First his erections stopped lasting; then his desire slowly faded. When he finally came in, he said it was "for his blood pressure." Only at the very end, with his hand already on the door, did he ask what had really brought him. We see that scene often, and the man almost always leaves relieved that he said it.

If something similar is happening to you, this article is for you. We won't repeat the list of checkups by age. This is about what comes before that: how to work up the nerve to say it, what happens inside the exam room, and why keeping quiet can cost you.

## Why so many men keep it to themselves

In many Hispanic families, men learned to tough it out. "It'll clear up by itself," "it's no big deal," "what will people think." Then there's language: explaining something this personal in English, searching for the right words, makes many men decide not to go at all. And there's the fear that it's something serious, or that someone at home or at work will find out.

None of those reasons make the symptom go away. What they do change is how late the cause is found.

## The things that are hardest to say (and often matter most)

These are the topics men bring up with the most embarrassment, and they are worth saying plainly:

- Erections that fail, don't last or don't happen.
- Less sex drive than before, with no clear reason.
- Pain, swelling or a small lump in a testicle.
- Changes when urinating: burning, trouble starting, a different flow or going more often.
- Discomfort in the lower belly, the groin or between the legs.
- Anything you notice "down there" that wasn't there before.

## What your first sentence can sound like

Getting started is the hardest part. Once you say it, the rest of the conversation flows. If you don't know how to begin, use one of these lines or put it in your own words:

| If what's going on is... | You can say something like... |
|---|---|
| Erections | "I have something about my sex life I'm embarrassed to ask." |
| Desire | "Lately I don't feel like it, and I want to know if that's normal." |
| A testicle | "I noticed something on a testicle and I want it checked." |
| Urination | "I've noticed changes when I pee for a few weeks now." |
| You can't even name it | "I have a problem in my private area and I'm not sure how to explain it." |

You don't need technical words. Say it the way you'd say it at home; the medical team at Clínica Hispana Nueva Salud is used to hearing it that way and will ask follow-up questions to understand better.

## What happens inside the exam room

The conversation is in Spanish from start to finish. These are the questions that usually come up, so nothing catches you off guard:

1. When it started, and whether it came on suddenly or little by little.
2. Whether it happens every time or only sometimes, and what makes it better or worse.
3. What conditions you have or have had: high blood pressure, high sugar, cholesterol, heart problems.
4. What medicines, vitamins or natural products you take, including ones you buy on your own.
5. How your sleep, stress, mood, drinking and smoking are.

Depending on what you share, the area may need to be checked: looking at and feeling the testicles or groin, or in some cases assessing the prostate. No step of the exam should take you by surprise. Before each one you can ask what will be done and why, ask whether it's needed today, and say you'd rather wait or talk it over first. Blood pressure and weight are checked too, and the team decides whether lab work or imaging makes sense.

## Your information stays in the visit

What you talk about in the exam room becomes part of your health record, and that information is private. In general terms, the clinic does not share it with your wife, your kids, your friends or your employer without your permission. If you're worried about something specific, such as who receives your results or which number they call, ask when you check in and say so clearly.

## Alone or with someone?

It's your call. Some men prefer to come alone so they can speak openly; others feel safer with their partner beside them, especially when the problem affects both of them. You can arrive with someone and ask for a few minutes alone during the visit. It's a normal request and nobody will think twice about it.

## Before you come: your three-item list

Nerves make it easy to forget what matters. Write it on your phone or a piece of paper:

- **What you feel:** the symptom in your own words, even if it seems minor.
- **Since when:** the approximate date you noticed it and whether it has changed.
- **What you take:** the name of each medicine or a photo of the boxes, since some can affect erections, desire or urination.

If it helps, also write down the question that worries you most and read it out as is. That way it doesn't stay at the door, like it almost did for don Arturo.

## Why it's not "just a bedroom issue"

This is the strongest reason not to stay silent. According to general medical information from sources like the NIH and MedlinePlus, erection problems are often linked to circulation. The blood vessels in the penis are small and can show damage before others do, so a failing erection can be one of the first clues of high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes or a heart that needs attention.

Something similar happens with other symptoms:

- **Urinating more often or being very thirsty** can be related to high blood sugar.
- **Low desire and constant tiredness** can have to do with sleep, mood, the thyroid or testosterone levels.
- **A lump in a testicle** deserves a prompt check, even if it doesn't hurt.

That's why the visit doesn't stop at the intimate symptom. It's often a chance to check your blood pressure, order [blood tests](/en/services/examenes-sangre) for glucose and cholesterol, or consider an [electrocardiogram](/en/services/electrocardiograma) when the case calls for it. If you already have diabetes or high blood pressure, follow-up through [chronic condition care](/en/services/condiciones-cronicas) can also help with these symptoms. If more is needed, specialist referral when results call for it is discussed, and each step is explained to you.

## When not to wait for a visit

Some situations aren't for booking a visit: call 911 or go straight to the emergency room.

- A testicle suddenly starts hurting intensely, with or without an injury, sometimes with nausea.
- You've gone hours without passing a single drop of urine and your bladder feels full and painful.
- You have a high fever, shaking chills and burning or pain when you urinate.
- You feel pressure or pain in your chest, shortness of breath or dizziness, even if it started during sex.
- You've had a painful erection for hours and it simply refuses to settle.
