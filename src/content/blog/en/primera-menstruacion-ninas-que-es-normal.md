It can happen on an ordinary Tuesday: your daughter walks out of the school restroom looking scared, or texts you from a friend's house, "Mom, I think I got it." For many families that moment shows up sooner than expected, and before anyone at home has really talked about it. If you grew up in a house where periods were never mentioned, it makes sense that you're not sure where to begin.

This guide is for the moms, dads, grandmothers and aunts raising a girl somewhere between 8 and 16. We'll go over the changes that announce a period is on its way, what the first months tend to look like, a few family myths worth letting go of, and the situations where it's wise to have the clinic's medical team take a look.

## The clues that come before the first period

Menstruation doesn't arrive out of nowhere. It's one of the later steps of puberty, and the body usually sends signals well ahead of time:

- **Breast buds.** A small, firm lump under one or both nipples is often the very first change. It can be a little tender and worry your daughter, but it's expected. The first period tends to follow about two to three years later.
- **Hair under the arms and in the pubic area**, plus stronger body odor that now calls for deodorant and a daily shower.
- **The growth spurt.** Many girls shoot up several inches in a single year, and once periods begin, that growth slows down.
- **White or clear discharge** in her underwear, without odor or itching. It usually shows up six months to a year before the first bleed and is a sign the body is getting ready.

In the United States most girls get their first period around 12 or 13, but anywhere from about 9 or 10 up to 15 is considered within the expected range, according to ACOG's guidance for teens ([ACOG](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/your-first-period)). Her mother's or older sister's experience gives a rough idea of when hers may come.

## What the first cycles usually look like

This is the part that worries parents most, and usually for no reason: **for the first year or two, periods are irregular.** The brain and the ovaries are still learning to work together, so one month the period may come after 25 days, then skip two months, then return.

For a teenager, the following is generally considered normal:

| What you notice | Range that's considered expected |
|---|---|
| Age at first period | Roughly between 10 and 15 |
| Days from one period to the next | 21 to 45 days, counting from the first day of bleeding |
| How long bleeding lasts | Up to 7 days |
| Changing pads | Every 3 to 6 hours on the heaviest days |
| Cramps | Lower-belly discomfort that eases with heat and rest and doesn't keep her home from school |

The color varies too: bright red, dark brown at the start or the end, sometimes a small clot. On its own, none of that points to a problem.

A practical tip: together, write down the first and last day of each period on a calendar or a phone app. After three or four months of notes, any visit becomes far more useful, because the medical team can see the pattern instead of relying on memory.

## Family myths worth retiring

Many Latino households pass down advice from grandmother to mother. Some of it is harmless, but some makes a girl feel ashamed or stop doing things that are good for her:

- **"Don't shower while you're on your period."** A warm shower is hygienic and actually soothes cramps.
- **"No cold drinks, lime or avocado."** No food delays or stops a period. What does help is eating beans, meat, eggs and leafy greens, which provide iron.
- **"No sports during your period."** Exercise often makes cramps milder. She can keep playing soccer or dancing folklórico.
- **"Now that it came, she's a woman."** Her body changed, but she's still a girl who needs information and company, not adult responsibilities.

Dads matter here as well. A father who buys pads without cracking jokes, or simply asks "how are you feeling?", teaches his daughter that her body is nothing to be embarrassed about.

## A backpack kit

Before the first period arrives, put together a small, discreet pouch for her backpack: two or three pads, a spare pair of underwear, wet wipes and a bag for stained clothes. Let her know the school nurse can help if it catches her by surprise.

## When to book a visit

Not everything is "just part of growing up." It's a good idea to bring her in if you notice any of the following:

- **No signs of puberty by age 13**, no period by 15, or no period three years after her breasts started developing.
- **Signs of puberty before age 8**, such as breast growth or pubic hair.
- **Periods closer than 21 days apart** or more than 45 days apart, or more than three months without bleeding after cycles had settled.
- **Bleeding that lasts longer than 7 days**, or that has her changing pads every one to two hours, or wearing two at once.
- **Tiredness, paleness or dizziness** during or after her period. Heavy bleeding can drain iron and lead to anemia.
- **Cramps that make her miss class** or don't improve with what the medical team has recommended.
- **Discharge with a bad odor, itching or burning** in the intimate area.

Heavy bleeding from the very first period deserves special attention: sometimes it's the first hint of a clotting problem no one in the family knew about, and blood work can help look into it.

## Warning signs: don't wait for an appointment

These situations can't wait for a regular visit. Head to the emergency room, or call **911**, when your daughter:

- soaks through a large pad every hour for two hours in a row or longer;
- feels dizzy when she stands up, faints, or looks very pale and weak;
- has severe belly pain that keeps her from walking, especially with fever or vomiting.
