Doña Lupe noticed it while drying her hair: the mole she had always had on the back of her neck caught on the comb and left a spot of blood on the towel. It didn't hurt, but it stayed on her mind. When had it gotten darker? Was it this size last year? She had no way to know, because she had never really looked at it.

That's the most common story with skin. Almost nobody knows what their moles used to look like, so when something changes there is nothing to compare it to. This guide helps you fix that, recognize the changes that do deserve a visit, and understand what happens when you come to the clinic with a mole that worries you.

## First, the reassuring part

Most adults have somewhere between 10 and 40 moles, and nearly all of them stay harmless for life. It's also normal for other marks that aren't moles to show up over the years: flat brown sun spots on the hands and face, rough bumps that look "stuck on" across the back or chest, or small skin tags on the neck and underarms. Many of these look odd and still aren't dangerous.

What matters isn't how many moles you have, but **whether one of them starts acting differently**. That's why the most useful tool is your own observation, done with a method.

## The questions we hear most

**"A mole of mine itches. Is that serious?"** A passing itch can come from clothing, sweat or dry skin. What stands out is a mole that itches again and again, for no clear reason, over several weeks.

**"It bled, but only because I nicked it shaving."** If you cut it and it healed well, that was probably all it was. If it bleeds on its own without being touched, or crusts over and keeps reopening, it's worth a look.

**"A new spot appeared and I'm over 40."** Fewer new moles show up after young adulthood. A spot that appears later in life and grows, or that looks unlike anything else on your skin, deserves a professional look.

**"I have darker skin. Do I need to check too?"** Yes. People with brown or dark skin burn less, but they aren't exempt. For them, worrying changes show up more often on the soles of the feet, the palms and under the nails, places almost nobody checks.

## The photo diary: your best point of comparison

Memory plays tricks; a photo doesn't. Build your own record with your phone:

1. **Pick good light.** Natural light near a window works better than the bathroom bulb.
2. **Take one wide shot and one close-up** of each mole you're watching: the wide one shows where it is, the close-up shows the detail.
3. **Place a size reference** next to it, like a ruler or a coin, so you can compare later.
4. **Keep the photos in their own album** labeled with the date and body location ("back, below the left shoulder blade").
5. **Repeat every month or two**, with the same light and the same distance.

With that album, when the medical team asks "has this changed?", you have the answer in your hand instead of a hunch.

## The mirror tour, head to toe

Choose a time right after a shower, with a full-length mirror and a hand mirror. If you live with someone, ask them to help with your back and scalp, and return the favor.

- **Face, ears and neck**, including behind the ears.
- **Scalp**, parting the hair with a comb or a blow dryer.
- **Chest, belly and, for women, under the breasts.**
- **Both sides of the arms, underarms, hands, palms and between the fingers.**
- **Back, buttocks and backs of the legs**, using both mirrors.
- **Soles of the feet, between the toes and all the nails**: a new or widening dark streak under a nail counts too.

You don't need to do this every day. Once a month is enough to get to know your skin.

## Changes that are worth a visit

Instead of memorizing rules, think about these concrete signs:

- **The "ugly duckling":** a mole that doesn't look like any of your others.
- **Growth** you can see when you compare it with your photos.
- **Color change:** it got darker, lighter in patches, or picked up red, bluish or gray tones.
- **Shape or border change:** it used to be round and now looks uneven or smudged outward.
- **New height:** a flat mole that starts to rise or feel firm.
- **Itching, burning or pain** that keeps coming back.
- **Bleeding, a crust or a sore** that never quite closes.
- **A new spot in an adult** that grows or looks different from everything else.

None of these changes is a diagnosis. They're reasons for someone with training to take a close look.

## Your mole visit on Bellfort, step by step

At Clínica Hispana Nueva Salud, a mole visit is in Spanish and unhurried. The medical team asks how long you've had it, what change you noticed, whether you've had a lot of sunburns and whether anyone in your family has had skin problems. Bringing your photo album helps a lot.

Then they examine the mole in good light and take the chance to check the rest of your skin, because sometimes the spot that worries you isn't the one that stands out most. From there, the next step is decided, and it may be one of these:

## When a specialized evaluation makes sense

As general guidance, some situations call for a specialized evaluation in addition to the visit at the clinic: a mole with several of the changes above at once, a dark streak under a nail that keeps widening, a sore that won't heal, having a very large number of moles, or a close relative who has had melanoma. Melanoma is a type of skin cancer that is less common than others, but it is easier to treat when found early, according to [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/melanoma.html). That's why changes shouldn't be brushed aside.

## Houston's sun doesn't take a break

The sun here is strong most of the year, in November too and on cloudy days. If you work in construction, landscaping, roofing or selling outdoors, your skin gets more sun than almost anyone's. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/skin-cancer/sun-safety/index.html) recommends:

- Seeking shade, especially around midday.
- Wearing long sleeves, a wide-brimmed hat and sunglasses.
- Applying broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 15 or higher, and reapplying at least every two hours and after sweating or swimming.
- Skipping tanning beds.

## Signs that mean the emergency room

A mole is almost never an emergency. But call **911** or go to the ER if a skin lesion keeps bleeding even when you press on it, or if the skin around it turns red and spreads quickly with fever, or red streaks run up the arm or leg.
